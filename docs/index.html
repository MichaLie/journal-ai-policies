Read this first

You have almost certainly not done anything wrong.

Of the 100 highest-ranked journals surveyed in 2023, 87% had published guidance on generative AI and just one prohibited using it to help write a manuscript outright. What nearly all of them prohibited was something different: listing AI as an author. Nothing in the records on this page penalises you for saying you used a tool — policies penalise not saying.

Ganjavi C, et al. Publishers' and journals' instructions to authors on use of generative artificial intelligence in academic and scientific publishing: bibliometric analysis. BMJ 2024;384:e077192. doi:10.1136/bmj-2023-077192 · PMID 38296328

Where the real lines are

The real lines are narrow, and they are not about tools. Do not invent citations. Do not present generated content as though it were your data. Do not put someone else's unpublished manuscript into a public AI tool while reviewing it. Those cause real harm. Using a model to improve your own sentences does not.

If you write in English as a second language, one more thing

Publishers screen submissions, and most do not say so. Across 17 cardiovascular journals studied, 15 used AI-detection software and one said so publicly. You should know that these detectors are unreliable, and unreliable in a specific direction: a widely cited study found they wrongly flagged 61% of essays written by non-native English speakers, against almost none by native speakers. That is a defect in the detectors, not in your writing. It is also why a clear, dated note of what you actually did is worth more to you than to anyone else.

Laffaye TA, et al. Artificial intelligence for manuscript writing: policies and implementation in cardiovascular journals. Cardiovasc Diagn Ther 2025. doi:10.21037/cdt-2025-381 · PMID 41216259

Liang W, et al. GPT detectors are biased against non-native English writers. Patterns 2023;4(7):100779. doi:10.1016/j.patter.2023.100779 · PMID 37521038

One major publisher rewrote its entire AI framework during 2026 with no announcement, no date stamp and no changelog. The guidance most publishers cite has not been revised since February 2023. People who work in research integrity full time find this confusing. You are not behind.

The common cases

Find what you did

"Fixing my English" and "rewriting my text" are not the same thing

This is the single most common question and the hardest boundary in the whole field, and it matters most to people writing in a second language. A test that works: did the meaning change, or only the wording? If the model corrected grammar, tightened phrasing or fixed article use, that is language editing, and almost nobody asks you to declare it. If it produced sentences carrying arguments, interpretations or conclusions you did not write yourself, that is drafting, and you should declare it. If you drafted in your own language and the model produced the English, that is translation — a third case, and one several publishers treat more leniently than drafting.

When you genuinely cannot tell, declare it. The records here show no penalty anywhere for declaring, and an explicit penalty in several places for not declaring.

Ans M, et al. The Presence and Nature of AI-Use Disclosure Statements in Medical Education Journals. Perspect Med Educ 2026;15:212-225. doi:10.5334/pme.2431 · PMID 41800434

I used AI to draft or substantially rewrite sectionsdeclare it

Declare it, naming the tool, the version and which sections. Most organisations recorded here permit this when it is declared. Two treat undeclared use of AI for core reasoning as a serious matter, so the declaration is what protects you.

example wording
[Tool and version] was used to draft [which sections], accessed [date]. All generated text was verified against the primary sources and substantially revised by the authors, who take full responsibility for the content.

Name the sections. A statement saying only "AI was used in preparing this manuscript" is the kind editors describe as uninformative — one study found disclosures averaging 43 words and describing the use only superficially.

I wrote in my own language and used AI to translatedeclare it

Declare it, and check the result. Organisations differ here more than anywhere else — one treats translation as the lowest-risk use there is, another asks that the output be checked by someone who reads both languages.

example wording
The manuscript was drafted in [language] and translated into English using [tool and version] on [date]. The translation was checked by [who checked it, and how], and revised by the authors.

If you have no bilingual colleague to ask — and many people do not — say what you did instead. For example, that you translated the English back into your own language and compared it against your original. Describing a real check honestly is worth more than naming a person who did not do one.

I let an AI agent run analyses, searches or multi-step work for medeclare it

Keep the log. This is the one case where you genuinely cannot fix things afterwards: prompts, model and version, dates, and the session records themselves. Most policies were written for a chatbot and ask only for a tool name — enough for one paragraph, meaningless for forty tool calls. Record more than you are asked for.

example wording
[Tool and version] was used as an agent to [what it executed: run analyses / search literature / generate and run code], between [dates]. Prompts and session transcripts are provided in [supplementary file]. Human verification was performed at [which checkpoints]; outputs that could not be independently reproduced were excluded. The authors take full responsibility for all reported results.

Most journals do not ask for this yet. Provide it anyway — not for compliance, but because an agentic pipeline cannot be reproduced from a tool name, and none of it can be reconstructed later.

I used AI to generate or retouch a figure showing my actual datacheck before you do it

Check before you do it. Experimental images — blots, micrographs, flow plots — are where prohibitions are real and where retractions actually happen. Adjusting brightness or contrast uniformly across a whole image is generally acceptable; anything that moves, adds or removes content is not.

example wording
[Tool and version] was used to produce the plots in Figure [n] from the underlying data, accessed [date]. No image content was added, removed or repositioned; source data are available at [location].

Only use this if it is true. Generating a chart from real data is routine; altering an experimental image is not, and that distinction is where retractions happen.

Things this page does not answer

  • Your specific journal. Every record here is at organisation level, and journals within one publisher genuinely differ — the records show a publisher whose own journals contradict each other three weeks apart. Check your target journal's author guidelines as well.
  • Your thesis. University rules bind you separately from journal rules, and they are often stricter. Ask your institution.
  • Your co-authors. If a co-author used AI and you did not, the declaration still covers the manuscript. Agree what it says before you submit — you are all signing it.
  • Your funder. Several funders have their own rules, and they moved in both directions during 2026. None is recorded here yet.
  • Preprint servers. They set their own policies, and they are not recorded here yet.
  • Summarising papers you then cite. No organisation recorded here addresses reading assistance. Verify what you cite and you are on solid ground.
  • Cover letters and responses to reviewers. Rarely addressed. One publisher here bans using AI to respond to reviewers; most say nothing.

What to actually do

Keep the record. That is the part you cannot recreate.

Start a log, even a rough one

Model and version, rough dates, the prompts, and the session records themselves. Everything else can be sorted out at submission — a disclosure statement takes ten minutes to write. A transcript from eight months ago cannot be rebuilt, and model versions get retired.

Already required, not hypothetical: ISTIC Guideline, Wiley asks for prompts, versions or archived session records today.

You already used it and kept no record

This is the common case and it is recoverable. Write down now what you can still remember — which tool, roughly which version, roughly when, and which parts of the work it touched. Do it today rather than at submission: model versions get retired and chat histories get deleted, and a rough note made now beats a perfect one written from memory later. Then describe it in your methods the way you would describe any other tool. You do not owe anyone an apology in that sentence.

You already found a fabricated reference in your own draft

Catching it before submission is the system working, not misconduct. Remove or replace the reference, check every remaining one against the actual record, and carry on. Nothing obliges you to report to anyone that you found and fixed an error in your own unsubmitted manuscript — that is simply what checking is. If it has already been published, contact the journal: a correction is routine.

Save a copy of the policy on the day you follow it

Policies here change without announcement, date stamp or changelog. One publisher rewrote its whole framework during 2026 with no public notice of any kind. If a rule changes between your writing and your submission, the burden of showing what it said falls on you — and the publisher keeps no public record.

It is also why this site stores a hash of every source. The snapshot is the receipt.

Confirm in advance only for these

Where a use is prohibited outright, the work cannot be fixed at submission. These are the ones worth checking first, with the organisations here that forbid them:

  • Using AI to draft manuscript textISTIC Guideline
  • AI-generated charts of your real dataISTIC Guideline
  • Presenting AI output as original dataSTM Association
  • Using AI when you peer-reviewScience / AAAS

Then check which page applies

A publisher can have more than one live policy, and they need not agree: one organisation here has a books policy that forbids what its journals policy permits, sitting behind a URL that ends “in-the-review-process”. Each record below marks the page to cite.

Method

What this does and does not claim

Word for word, or not at all

Every verdict carries a short quote, its source, and how it was obtained. All 50 quotes on this page are checked automatically against the stored copy of the page they came from; the build fails if any quote cannot be found. An earlier version of this site carried a sentence in quotation marks that turned out to be a research assistant’s paraphrase. That is what this check exists to prevent.

Published policy only

This records what organisations have published. It cannot see what editors actually do. Across seventeen cardiovascular journals studied, fifteen ran AI-detection software and one said so publicly — the gap between stated and practised is real, and this site does not close it.

Coverage, stated plainly

10 organisations, of which nine are Anglophone and one is not. Journal-level records, funders, preprint servers and content licensing are not covered yet. If your funder or institution is not here, that means we have not looked — not that it is silent.

Where the bytes come from

13 of 16 sources are fetched directly. 3 return a block page or a challenge to any automated client, so those records are checked against dated Internet Archive captures instead, and each says so. Nothing is recorded from memory.

Contradictions, counted honestly

1 substantive internal contradiction and 2 dating or tagging oddities across 10 organisations. A stale HTML title tag is not a policy contradiction and is not counted as one. Research notes record more contradictions among organisations not yet recorded here.

0 cells still await a quote

Verdicts recorded without captured wording are marked and published, never asserted as verified. Where we could not find wording at all, the cell was changed to “silent” rather than left as a claim.

This page declares itself unmaintained automatically once its oldest record passes 550 days without re-checking. The closest precedent to this project still serves a live page, with no staleness warning, four years after its last update.

Corrections and right of reply

If you work for an organisation recorded here and think a record is wrong, we want to fix it. Every record names its source and quotes the wording it relies on, so corrections can be specific. Open an issue and we will respond. Corrected records carry the date they changed, and the change stays in the public history.
